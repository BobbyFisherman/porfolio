VISION VOICE

Wearable ASL-to-English Translation Glasses

Vision Voice is a pair of smart glasses that watches a signer's hands through a built-in camera, recognizes American Sign Language in real time, and translates it into English — outputting the result as spoken audio through temple speakers, projected text on the lens, or both. It is designed for deaf and hard-of-hearing users who struggle with lip reading and need a hands-free, always-on way to understand the people around them.

01

The Problem

◎
The Gap
Over 500,000 people in the United States use American Sign Language as their primary language. But the vast majority of hearing people do not understand ASL. When a deaf person is in a conversation with someone who doesn't sign, they are often forced to rely on lip reading — which is unreliable, exhausting, and only about 30% accurate even in ideal conditions.
⚡
The Solution
Vision Voice is a pair of lightweight glasses with a tiny built-in camera. When someone signs in front of the wearer, the camera captures their hand movements, a machine learning model identifies what word they are signing, and the translation is delivered instantly — either as audio through speakers in the temples of the glasses, as text projected onto the bottom of the lens, or both at the same time. No phone screen. No interpreter. Just glasses.
▲
The Goal
Build a working wearable prototype within one month on a roughly $150 budget that can recognize 50 to 100 ASL words — enough to carry a basic conversation. The glasses should be comfortable enough to wear like normal glasses, with all processing handled by a small pocket unit connected wirelessly. The prototype will be demonstrated at the VHHS 24-hour hackathon.

Step 1 — Capture
A Raspberry Pi Camera Module (ZeroCam) is mounted on the glasses frame. It continuously captures video of the person in front of the wearer, focused on their hands and upper body where ASL signs occur.
Step 2 — Detect
Each video frame is processed using OpenCV to detect and extract hand landmarks — 21 individual points per hand that map the shape, position, and orientation of every finger and the palm. These landmarks are what the machine learning model uses to understand the sign, not the raw image itself.
Step 3 — Transmit
The landmark data is sent wirelessly from the glasses to a pocket-sized processing unit the wearer carries. The current plan is to use an ESP32 microcontroller on the glasses side, which encodes the landmark coordinates and transmits them over a short-range wireless link. Only landmark data is sent — not full images — keeping the data small and the connection fast.
Step 4 — Classify
The pocket unit runs a Raspberry Pi 5 (8GB) with a custom-trained machine learning model. The model receives the hand landmark coordinates and classifies them as a specific ASL word. Each word in the vocabulary is trained on roughly 100 sample recordings of the sign being performed in different positions, angles, and distances — so the model can recognize the sign even if the signer's hands are slightly higher, lower, closer, or farther apart than the training data.
Step 5 — Output
The recognized word is delivered to the wearer through two channels. Small speakers built into the temples of the glasses play the word as audio. At the same time, a micro-projector mounted behind the lens projects the word as text onto the bottom of the lens — visible only to the wearer, like a heads-up display. If the wearer is deaf, they can disable audio and use only text. If they are blind, they can disable text and use only audio. By default both run simultaneously.

Hardware

The physical design is inspired by Meta Ray-Ban smart glasses — the goal is a normal-looking pair of glasses that someone could wear all day without drawing attention. The frame will be 3D printed in black PETG, chosen for its durability, slight flexibility, heat resistance, and lightweight feel compared to PLA. The frame will go through multiple print iterations to get the fit, weight distribution, and component clearances right.
📷 ZeroCam (Raspberry Pi Camera Module)
Captures video of signer's hands
Small camera module mounted on the glasses frame — either on the bridge (nose area) for the best centered view of the signer's hands and face, or on the side temple like Meta glasses for easier internal wiring. Final placement will be decided during prototyping based on field of view and wiring constraints.
Planned — camera selected, mount position in testing
📡 ESP32 Microcontroller
On-glasses processor and wireless transmitter
Sits inside the glasses frame. Receives raw camera frames, runs OpenCV hand landmark detection to extract 21-point hand coordinates, then transmits only the lightweight landmark data wirelessly to the pocket processing unit. By processing on the glasses and sending only coordinates instead of full images, the wireless link stays fast and low-latency.
Planned — evaluating ESP32 vs dedicated micro-transmitter
🧠 Raspberry Pi 5 (8GB)
Pocket processing unit — runs ML classification
The brain of the system. Carried in the wearer's pocket or clipped nearby. Receives hand landmark data wirelessly from the glasses, runs the trained machine learning model to classify the ASL sign, then sends the recognized word back to the glasses for audio and text output. The 8GB model was chosen for enough memory to run the ML model smoothly.
Available — provided as existing hardware
🔊 Temple Speakers
Audio output — speaks the translated word
Small, budget-friendly speakers mounted inside the temple arms of the glasses, pointed toward the wearer's ears. When a sign is recognized, the word is played as spoken audio. Speaker selection will prioritize reliability, small size, and low power draw within budget.
Planned — researching small reliable drivers
💡 Micro-Projector + Lens
Text output — projects translated word onto lens
A tiny projector mounted behind and slightly above the lens projects text onto the bottom portion of the lens. The projection is only visible to the wearer — like a heads-up display. By projecting onto the bottom of the lens, the projection distance is minimized and the text stays out of the wearer's main line of sight until they glance down.
Planned — researching micro-projector options within budget
🖨️ 3D Printed Frame (PETG)
Houses all components in a wearable form factor
Custom-designed frame modeled to hold the camera, ESP32, speakers, and projector while remaining lightweight and comfortable. Printed in black PETG for its balance of strength, slight flex (won't snap when put on or taken off), heat resistance (won't warp from electronics), and smooth finish. Multiple iterations expected to nail the fit.
Planned — CAD design in progress

04

Software

01 Hand Landmark Detection
OpenCV is used to process each camera frame and identify hand landmarks. For every hand in frame, it extracts 21 coordinate points — the tip, knuckle, and base of each finger, plus the wrist. These 21 points create a skeleton map of the hand's shape and position. This is the raw data the ML model uses. The model never sees the actual image — only the landmark coordinates.
OpenCV 21 Landmarks Per Hand Real-Time
02 Custom ML Model
The machine learning model is being trained from scratch — not fine-tuned from an existing model. For each ASL word in the vocabulary, roughly 100 sample recordings are captured showing the sign performed at different heights, distances, angles, and hand spacings. This variation is intentional — it teaches the model to recognize the sign no matter how the signer holds their hands, making it work in real-world conditions where no two people sign exactly the same way.
Trained From Scratch ~100 Samples Per Word Variation-Tolerant
03 Output Engine
Once the model classifies a sign, the recognized English word is sent back to the glasses. The output engine handles two channels: text-to-speech for the temple speakers, and text rendering for the lens projector. The wearer can configure which outputs are active from the pocket unit — both by default, audio-only for blind users, or text-only for deaf users.
Text-to-Speech Lens Projection Configurable Output
04 Language Support
The initial prototype outputs translations in English. Because the system translates ASL signs into text first and then converts that text to audio, adding support for other output languages in the future would only require swapping the text-to-speech language — the hand detection and sign classification pipeline would remain the same.

Current Progress

Vision Voice started as a concept at the VHHS AI Hackathon 2025 where it placed 3rd. The hackathon demo was a proof-of-concept — a camera connected to a laptop running code that could detect thumbs-up and thumbs-down gestures and speak the result through speakers. The judges encouraged continuing the project. It is now being developed as a full wearable prototype for the upcoming VHHS 24-hour hackathon in approximately one month.
